# Semantic storyboard — airline overbooking explainer

No hand-authored timecodes. Build scene timing from the final aligned narration and word cues.

## Scene A — 180 seats / 185 tickets
Anchor: "Your plane has 180 seats" → "They did it on purpose."

Start on a clean top-down plane cabin. Seat counter races to 180. A ticket counter continues to 181, 182, 183, 184, 185. The five extra ticket cards try to enter the full seat grid and stack awkwardly outside it.

On "No, nobody failed kindergarten," briefly show a childlike 180 < 185 equation stamped CORRECT, then immediately snap back to the premium editorial system. Keep the joke dry, not goofy.

## Scene B — the inventory expires
Anchor: "some passengers cancel late" → "worth exactly zero."

Show a row of booked passenger icons. A few fade before boarding. Isolate one empty seat. A departure clock sweeps forward; at the moment the plane icon leaves, a price tag on the empty seat flips to $0.

Core visual idea: an airline seat is expiring inventory.

## Scene C — the forecast
Anchor: "Airlines know this" → "probably won't board."

Transform recent-flight cards into a simple forecasting panel: route, weekday, booking history, cancellations, no-shows. Do not mimic a real airline dashboard or imply a universal formula. Several historical dots resolve into a simple forecast bubble: "≈ 5 no-shows" for this hypothetical flight.

## Scene D — when the model works
Anchor: "Imagine a 180-seat flight" → "The spreadsheet gets a tiny promotion."

Animate 185 ticket cards into the system. Five passenger icons peel away before the gate. The remaining 180 click neatly into the 180-seat grid. Hold for one satisfying beat. A tiny spreadsheet icon gets a miniature upward arrow/badge on the punchline.

## Scene E — three possible realities
Anchor: "But predictions are not promises" → "starts sounding like an auction."

Run three rapid side-by-side or sequential simulations using the same 185 bookings:
- 7 no-shows → 178 occupied, 2 empty seats.
- 5 no-shows → 180 occupied, perfect fill.
- 0 no-shows → 180 occupied + 5 standing passenger icons.

On the final case, freeze the five extras outside the cabin and reveal a small caption: "statistical error has entered the chat."

Then cut to an airport gate board with an offer counter ready at $200.

## Scene F — the pressure-release valve
Anchor: "First, airlines may ask for volunteers" → "never liked this destination anyway."

Use a stylized gate scene. Offer counter rises $200 → $400. Passenger silhouettes glance at one another. One hand slowly rises at the punchline. Add a tiny suitcase beside that passenger already rolling toward the exit before the hand is fully up.

Show a compact jurisdiction note on the U.S. rule only while the narration explicitly says "In the United States". Avoid legal-detail clutter.

## Scene G — the actual optimization problem
Anchor: "So why risk paying people at all?" → "expected overbooking cost."

Build a clean two-sided balance:
LEFT: empty seat departs → revenue opportunity disappears.
RIGHT: too many passengers → compensation / reaccommodation cost.

The visual should make clear that the airline is choosing a forecasted tradeoff, not blindly maximizing ticket count. Avoid equations unless they are extremely simple and decorative.

## Scene H — every flight behaves differently
Anchor: "And the number is not the same" → "forecast, not a rule."

Morph one generic flight card into multiple contexts: Monday business route, holiday flight, different demand curves. The hypothetical "extra tickets" number changes, then one card lands on "0 extra" to support "Some airlines don't oversell at all."

## Scene I — the deeper reveal
Anchor: "So the weird truth is this" → end.

Start close on seat 22A. Pull back: the seat becomes one node inside a field of probability dots representing booked passengers. Some dots fade before reaching a gate line; others arrive.

Collapse the model back into a simple plane silhouette as the final line lands:
"They counted everyone — including the people they expect not to show up."

End immediately. No long outro. Leave only enough tail for the final idea to register.
